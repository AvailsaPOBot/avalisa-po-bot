Why bots get catchy names

A name like "Lyra" or "Neuro" sounds like a product with a research team behind it. Often it is simply the label on an offer: a Telegram channel, a signals subscription, a downloadable script or a browser extension. The name tells you nothing about:

  • What it actually is: a signal list, a script you run yourself, or an extension that acts in your browser.
  • How it places trades: whether anything trades at all, or whether you click every trade yourself after a signal.
  • Who is accountable: whether there is a real company, a support address and a refund path, or only an ad account.

Judge the mechanism, not the label.

Five questions to ask about any named bot

  1. Who makes it? Is there a company name, a website, a store listing and a support address? You can check a Chrome Web Store listing's publisher, reviews and permissions before installing; see how to check a Chrome Web Store trading bot listing.
  2. How does it place trades? Does it operate the trading page in your own browser, send you signals to click yourself, or claim to trade somewhere else? Ask what happens after a loss: Martingale strategies raise the stake. In Avalisa's panel, Steps N means up to N+1 trades in a losing cycle before it resets to the start amount.
  3. What does it need access to? Does it ask for your Pocket Option login, your card details or files on your computer? An extension that works on your signed-in tab should not need your Pocket Option password.
  4. How do you stop it? Can you disable or uninstall it yourself, and does that stop trading straight away? You should never depend on emailing a seller to stop your money moving; see how to stop or remove the bot safely.
  5. What does it cost? One-time or recurring? How do you cancel? Watch for a cheap first month that turns into an auto-renewing subscription.

Red flags in the pitch

  • Results screenshots. Balance screenshots are easy to make and to cherry-pick. Ask how they were produced and whether they include losses.
  • Urgency. "Offer ends tonight" or "only a few licences left". A trading tool does not need a countdown clock.
  • Paid signals sold as certainty. If the product is really "we tell you when to click", you are buying opinions, not automation, and you still place every trade.
  • The name doing the selling. The more the pitch leans on the brand, the less it says about the mechanism. Keep asking the five questions until the answers are concrete.
